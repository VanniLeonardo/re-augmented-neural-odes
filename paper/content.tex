\section{Scope of reproducibility}
\label{sec:scope}

Dupont et al.\supercite{dupont2019augmented} argue that a Neural ODE is limited in a way that is
structural rather than statistical. The flow of an ODE is a homeomorphism of the state space, so
the map it can represent cannot alter the topology of the input: trajectories cannot cross, and
features that are nested or interleaved in the input cannot be pulled apart. Their remedy,
\emph{augmentation}, appends extra zero-valued dimensions to the state, giving the flow room to
separate the data in a higher-dimensional space. The paper supports this with a topological
argument, a cost signature (the number of function evaluations, NFE, that the adaptive solver
needs), and image benchmarks at matched parameter counts.

We test the following claims. Claims 1--4 are from Dupont et al.; claims 5--8 are the
solver and memory claims of Chen et al.\supercite{chen2018neural} on which the first paper builds.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A one-dimensional Neural ODE cannot represent the crossing map.} The flow of a
        scalar ODE is order-preserving, so no Neural ODE can approximate
        $g(x)=+1$ for $x<0$ and $g(x)=-1$ for $x>0$; a single augmented dimension suffices.
        (Proposition 1 and Figure 3.)
  \item \textbf{The cost of a Neural ODE grows as it trains, while an augmented one stays flat.}
        On a task requiring the flow to separate nested classes, the NFE required by the
        Neural ODE increases with the training budget, and the augmented model is both cheaper
        and flatter. (Section 4.2 and Figure 6.)
  \item \textbf{Neural ODEs generalise poorly across a region absent from training.} Removing an
        angular wedge from the training set leaves a large generalisation gap on that wedge for
        the Neural ODE, while the augmented model generalises across it.
        (Section 5.1 and Figure 9.)
  \item \textbf{At matched parameter counts, augmentation improves image accuracy and reduces
        cost.} On MNIST and CIFAR-10, the augmented model reaches higher test accuracy than a
        parameter-matched Neural ODE, at lower NFE. (Table 1.)
  \item \textbf{Solver error falls and cost rises as the tolerance tightens}, with forward
        computation time approximately proportional to NFE. (Chen et al., Figure 3a--b.)
  \item \textbf{The backward pass costs about half the forward pass in function evaluations.}
        (Chen et al., Figure 3c.)
  \item \textbf{The number of function evaluations increases over the course of training.}
        (Chen et al., Figure 3d.)
  \item \textbf{The adjoint method's memory cost is constant in the effective depth of the
        model.} (Chen et al., Table 1, memory column.)
\end{enumerate}

Claims 1--3 are qualitative and mechanistic; claims 4--8 are quantitative, and for those the
tolerance at which the ODE is solved determines whether the measurement means anything at all.
Section~\ref{sec:recon} makes that precise, because it governs every number we report.

We do not attempt Chen et al.'s Table 1 benchmark results: our MNIST models are our own seeded
baselines and are not comparable to that table's architecture, budget or solver. We also exclude
the Latent ODE and ODE-RNN models of Rubanova et al.\supercite{rubanova2019latent}, and the SVHN
and ImageNet experiments of the original paper, none of which are needed to test the claims
above and all of which exceeded the compute available to us.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Model descriptions}

All models integrate a learned vector field $f_\theta$ from $t=0$ to $t=1$ and classify the
terminal state with a \emph{single linear map}. The linear readout matters: a nonlinear head
could separate classes by itself, and the claims under test are about what the flow can do.
An augmented model (ANODE) appends $p$ zero-valued dimensions to the state before integration;
$p=0$ recovers the plain Neural ODE (NODE).

For the two-dimensional tasks, $f_\theta$ is a multilayer perceptron of width 32 taking the state
and time, applied directly to the raw input, following Appendix F.1.1 of the original paper. No
encoder precedes the flow: a learned encoder is itself a diffeomorphism and would hand the model
a warp that the topological argument assumes it does not have.

For the image tasks, $f_\theta$ is the convolutional field of Appendix F.1.2 --- a
$1\times1$, $3\times3$, $1\times1$ stack --- with the time injected as an additional channel
\emph{before every convolution}, and augmentation realised as $p$ extra zero channels on the
input. Model capacity is matched between NODE and ANODE by choosing the number of filters, as the
original does: on MNIST 92 filters (85{,}316 parameters) against 64 filters with $p=5$
(85{,}462); on CIFAR-10, 125 filters (173{,}611) against 64 filters with $p=10$ (172{,}452). Our
counts sit about 0.5--1\% above the counts stated in the original Table 1, a difference we trace
to the flattening readout and document in Section~\ref{sec:deviations}. The harness asserts the
two arms are matched to within 2\% before every run.

Everything was written from the descriptions in the two papers. We did not consult or copy either
author's released implementation; the solver library \texttt{torchdiffeq}\supercite{torchdiffeq}
is used only to integrate, and its adaptive Dormand--Prince (\texttt{dopri5}) method is the
default throughout unless a claim concerns the solver itself.

\subsection{Datasets}

The toy task is the concentric-spheres dataset of Appendix F.2.1: a filled inner disk
($\lVert x\rVert \le 0.5$) enclosed by an annulus ($1.0 \le \lVert x\rVert \le 1.5$), sampled in a
$1{:}2$ ratio. Because the inner class is entirely surrounded by the outer one, no flow in the
plane can separate them without the trajectories crossing --- the geometry is the argument. We
also use a second, thinner concentric-circles geometry as a robustness check where noted.

The image tasks use MNIST and CIFAR-10 with per-image scaling to $[0,1]$ and no data
augmentation, so that the comparison is about the flow rather than about an augmentation
pipeline. Neither dataset is redistributed with our code; the loaders fetch them on first use.

\subsection{Solver tolerance and the reconstruction check}
\label{sec:recon}

An adaptive ODE solver takes a tolerance and returns a trajectory together with the number of
function evaluations it used. Neither output announces failure. If the tolerance is too loose for
the field being integrated, the solver still returns a plausible-looking state and a plausible
NFE, and every quantity derived from them --- accuracy, cost, the comparison between two models
--- describes the solver's shortcut rather than the model. This matters especially here, because
the vector field of a Neural ODE \emph{stiffens as it trains}: the tolerance that integrated the
field at initialisation need not integrate it later, so a fixed tolerance silently changes meaning
over the course of a run.

We therefore treat the solver tolerance as an experimental axis rather than a default, and attach
a check to every measurement. Because the flow of an ODE is invertible, integrating forward from
$0$ to $T$ and then back from $T$ to $0$ must return the input. We integrate in both directions
with the same solver and tolerance and record the relative reconstruction error
\[
  \varepsilon_{\text{recon}}
  \;=\;
  \frac{\lVert \phi_{T\rightarrow 0}(\phi_{0\rightarrow T}(x)) - x \rVert}{\lVert x \rVert},
\]
flagging a measurement as faithful (\texttt{recon\_ok}) when $\varepsilon_{\text{recon}} < 10^{-2}$.
This is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one: it detects a solver that is not tracking the
flow, and it is cheap enough to run on every row.

Two rules follow, and we apply them throughout. First, a quantity compared between two models is
reported at the \emph{loosest tolerance at which both models pass the check on every seed}, so
that neither is flattered by a tolerance that suits only it. Second, rows that fail the check are
kept and reported rather than discarded: they are evidence about the tolerance axis, and several
of our findings are visible only in them.

The consequences are not hypothetical. Measured at the solver library's default tolerance of
$10^{-3}$, our one-dimensional crossing-flow experiment fails the check for all ten trained
models, the worst reconstructing its input with a relative error above $10$; the conclusion
happens to survive re-measurement, but at that tolerance it had not been established. In the
image experiments the check fails at the tolerance used for \emph{training} on every model and
seed, which we return to in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Several conclusions reached earlier in
this project were retracted once the check was introduced.

\subsection{Deviations from the original descriptions}
\label{sec:deviations}

We reimplemented from the text, and the text does not fix everything. Where a choice was
unstated we made one and recorded it; where we knowingly departed from a stated choice we say so
here. Table~\ref{tab:deviations} lists the departures that could plausibly move a number. A
fuller register, including departures we judged immaterial and several we corrected during the
work, accompanies the code.

Three deserve emphasis. First, we train at a solver tolerance of $10^{-3}$ while the original
uses the solver library's defaults, which are far tighter; this is a concession to compute, and
Section~\ref{sec:recon} is precisely the machinery that keeps it honest, since we re-measure at
tolerances that pass the reconstruction check rather than trusting the training tolerance.
Second, we run five seeds against the original's twenty repeats on the toy task, so our
uncertainty intervals are wider than the original's and small differences between arms should not
be over-read. Third, for the secondary claims we integrate with an explicit Runge--Kutta method
(\texttt{dopri5}) where Chen et al. used implicit Adams; that is a deliberate choice --- it is the
method a reader reaching for \texttt{torchdiffeq} today will use --- but it is not a neutral one,
and it turns out to matter for claim 6.

\subsection{Experimental setup and code}

Every experiment runs at least five seeds; the held-out-wedge metrics of claim 3 are strongly
skewed across seeds, so those use ten. We report the mean and standard deviation for image
accuracy, matching the original's convention, and the median with interquartile range wherever
the across-seed distribution is skewed enough that a mean would misrepresent it. Which statistic
is used is stated with each result.

Before each run we recorded, in the repository's log, the condition under which the claim would
be considered refuted --- written down in advance, so that the raw observation decides the outcome
rather than a judgement made after seeing it. Those pre-registered conditions are quoted with the
results below, including the two occasions on which our own prediction turned out to be wrong.
Every result row also records the hardware that produced it; this is not bookkeeping for its own
sake, and Section~\ref{sec:results} gives a case where it caught a confound.

The code, the per-seed result files and the scripts that turn them into every figure and number
in this article are in the repository cited at the end. A single command rebuilds all figures from
the committed results without a GPU, and the continuous-integration workflow runs that same path
on every commit.

\subsection{Computational requirements}

Experiments ran on one NVIDIA RTX 3090 (24 GB) and on A100 80 GB MIG slices of a SLURM cluster;
the two-dimensional tasks are faster on CPU and were run there, on an AMD Ryzen 7 7700X. The
matched-parameter MNIST experiment takes about 1.9 hours and CIFAR-10 about 4.3 hours for five
seeds on one A100 slice; the systematic extension of claim 3 takes about 1.6 hours across
fourteen CPU workers. The whole set of experiments is on the order of twenty GPU-hours, which we
note deliberately: none of the claims tested here requires a large compute budget to check.

Seeding fixes initialisation, data generation and shuffling, so a re-run reproduces our numbers
within the spread we report rather than bit-for-bit; adaptive solvers and GPU kernel selection are
not bit-reproducible across machines. Figures rebuilt from the committed result files are exactly
reproducible, and one toy experiment re-run two months later on a different thread count returned
its held-out accuracies unchanged.

\subsection{Results reproducing Dupont et al.}

\subsubsection{Claim 1: the one-dimensional crossing flow}

Proposition 1 predicts more than failure: an order-preserving map that must send both classes to
opposite constants can do no better than collapsing them onto a single value, which for targets
$\pm 1$ gives a mean squared error of exactly 1. That number is the theoretical floor, and it is
what distinguishes a model that is genuinely obstructed from one that is merely undertrained.

At the loosest tolerance where both arms pass the reconstruction check on every seed ($10^{-7}$),
the Neural ODE reaches a median MSE of \textbf{1.0002} (range 1.0000--1.0003 over five seeds) and
the augmented model \textbf{0.0007} (0.0001--0.0013). The Neural ODE sits \emph{at} the floor, not
below it: the claim is reproduced, and reproduced in the strong form, since the failure has the
magnitude the theory predicts rather than merely being large.

This experiment is also the clearest illustration of Section~\ref{sec:recon}. Measured at the
solver library's default tolerance of $10^{-3}$, the reconstruction check fails for all ten
trained models, the worst reconstructing its input with a relative error above 10. The MSE values
there are close to those above, so the conclusion is robust --- but at that tolerance it had not
been \emph{established}, and robustness one has not checked is an assumption. Interestingly the
augmented model is also the easier one to integrate: it passes the check from $10^{-5}$, whereas
the Neural ODE, whose flow must steepen to approximate a map it cannot represent, needs $10^{-7}$.

\subsubsection{Claim 2: cost growth with training budget}

Sweeping the training budget from 25 to 1000 epochs and measuring at a reconstruction-checked
tolerance of $10^{-6}$, the Neural ODE's median forward NFE grows by a factor of \textbf{1.74} on
the concentric spheres while the augmented model's grows by \textbf{1.04}; at the original's
budget of 50 epochs the Neural ODE needs \textbf{218} function evaluations against the augmented
model's \textbf{170}. Both models classify the data essentially perfectly at that budget (dense
accuracy 0.996 and 1.000), which is the point: the two models are not separated by accuracy but
by the cost of the flow that achieves it. The pattern repeats on the thinner circles geometry
(growth $\times 1.37$ against $\times 1.04$), so it is not an artefact of one dataset.

One caveat belongs here rather than in a footnote. On the spheres, three of sixty measurements
fail the reconstruction check --- a single seed at the three largest budgets, whose flow becomes
too stiff to integrate at $10^{-6}$. That seed is the most extreme case of exactly the effect
under study, so we report the medians including it and note that it cannot be measured faithfully
at this tolerance.

\subsubsection{Claim 3: generalisation across an unobserved region}

Removing the angular wedge $[0, \pi/5]$ from the training set and testing on that wedge alone
separates the two models sharply. Over five seeds, all reconstruction-checked, the Neural ODE
reaches a median accuracy of \textbf{0.619} on the held-out wedge with a loss of \textbf{6.089},
while the augmented model reaches \textbf{1.000} and \textbf{0.000}. Both fit the training data
perfectly, and both score above 0.96 on an ordinary validation set; the gap appears only on the
region the training data never covered.

The across-seed spread deserves stating. The Neural ODE's held-out accuracy has an interquartile
range of 0.613--0.921: most seeds collapse to near chance on the wedge, but one seed threads it
almost perfectly. A mean would misrepresent this, which is why we report medians and why the
extension below uses ten seeds rather than five.

\paragraph{Extension: how the gap depends on the missing region.}
The original demonstrates this with one wedge on one geometry. We swept the augmentation
dimension $p \in \{0,1,2,3,5\}$, the wedge width $\{\pi/8, \pi/5, \pi/3\}$ and two geometries over
ten seeds --- 300 runs, each with the same reconstruction check, of which six fail it and are
excluded. The augmented model generalises better at every width, on both geometries, for every
$p \ge 1$; no pre-registered check failed.

The interesting part is where the effect stops growing. On the spheres the advantage in held-out
accuracy increases from $+0.07$ at $\pi/8$ to $+0.26$ at $\pi/5$, and then flattens ($+0.27$ to
$+0.30$ at $\pi/3$), with the last step lying inside the across-seed spread. Measured by loss
rather than accuracy the advantage actually \emph{shrinks} at the widest wedge, because the
augmented model degrades there too: a large enough hole hurts both models, and augmentation does
not make an unobserved region free. On the second geometry the gap is smaller and not monotone in
width. Two further observations: every model, the Neural ODE included, scores at least 0.998 on
the observed region, so the failure is specifically an inability to extrapolate into the gap
rather than a general fitting problem; and more augmentation is not monotonically better, with
$p=2$ generalising best at the widest wedge on both geometries.

\subsubsection{Claim 4: matched-parameter image benchmarks}

Table~\ref{tab:table1} gives our matched-parameter results beside the original's, each quoted at
the loosest tolerance where both arms pass the reconstruction check on every seed. The ordering
the claim asserts is reproduced on both datasets: the augmented model is more accurate, by
3.9 percentage points on MNIST and 6.4 on CIFAR-10, and it is also cheaper, requiring
1.65--2.10 times fewer function evaluations on MNIST and 1.24--1.93 times fewer on CIFAR-10
across the faithful tolerances and across two independent runs. We give those as ranges
deliberately: the accuracy figures are stable across runs, but the NFE ratio is not, and quoting
a single number for it would suggest a precision we do not have.

Two of the four absolute numbers land on the original's and two undershoot. The CIFAR-10 Neural
ODE matches within noise. The MNIST augmented model matches. The MNIST Neural ODE falls about
2.2 points short, consistently across two independent runs, and the CIFAR-10 augmented model
falls 0.6 points short in the run reported here and 1.3 points short in an earlier run on
different hardware --- a spread that is itself larger than the shortfall, so we report it as a
small undershoot rather than claiming either a match or a discrepancy. The most likely
explanations are the reduced epoch budgets and the five-seed sample (Section~\ref{sec:deviations});
we did not tune towards the published values, and the pre-registered conditions concerned the
ordering, which holds.

\paragraph{The tolerance the accuracies are measured at.}
These models are \emph{trained} at $10^{-3}$, and at that tolerance the reconstruction check fails
for every model and every seed on both datasets. Our first version of this experiment measured
accuracy there, which meant the headline numbers came from a regime in which the solver was not
tracking the flow. We therefore re-ran both experiments with the training tolerance included in
the evaluation ladder and the test set re-evaluated at every tolerance. Accuracy turns out to be
almost insensitive to this: it moves by at most 0.11 points on average and 0.37 points for the
worst single seed between the training tolerance and a checked one. The cost measurements are a
different matter --- the forward NFE at $10^{-7}$ is several times its value at $10^{-3}$ --- which
is why we quote accuracy and cost at checked tolerances throughout, and why the distinction is
worth making even when, as here, the conclusion does not change.

\subsection{Results reproducing Chen et al.}

\subsubsection{Claim 5: solver dynamics}

We trained a convolutional ODE-Net, froze it, and swept the solver at evaluation, measuring the
endpoint error against a high-accuracy reference (an eighth-order method at $10^{-8}$), the
forward wall-clock, and the function evaluations. Both halves of the claim hold. Error falls
monotonically as the tolerance tightens, for every adaptive solver tested. Forward time is
strongly rank-correlated with NFE: Spearman's $\rho$ between 0.983 and 0.992 per solver.

The cost half of the claim, however, was pre-registered as \emph{monotone} and by that condition
it is refuted: \texttt{dopri5}'s median NFE falls from 26 to 20 between tolerances $10^{-1}$ and
$10^{-2}$. We report the refutation as it stands rather than restating the claim to fit. Where the
violation lies is nonetheless informative --- both of those rows fail the reconstruction check on
every seed, so the step sizes the controller chooses there are not tracking the flow, and among
rows that do pass the check the cost is monotone. The honest summary is that cost rises with
tolerance wherever the solver is actually integrating, and behaves erratically where it is not.

Two observations came out of this experiment that bear on the rest of the article. First, the
reference itself is expensive in a revealing way: its cost grows by a factor of 5--7 per decade of
tolerance, where an eighth-order method on a non-stiff field should cost roughly $1.3\times$ per
decade. That gap is a direct measurement of the stiffness of a trained Neural ODE field, and it is
why high-accuracy references on these models are costly. Second, a nominal tolerance is not
comparable across solvers: at $10^{-5}$, \texttt{dopri5} spends 116 function evaluations and lands
just at the edge of the reconstruction check, while \texttt{bosh3} spends 227 and \texttt{dopri8}
1055 for reconstruction errors an order of magnitude smaller. The tolerance is a request, not a
guarantee; the reconstruction check is what makes the comparison meaningful.

Finally, this experiment is where recording hardware per row earned itself. The five seeds were
scheduled across two different GPU partition sizes, so the pooled wall-clock mixed machines.
Re-checking the time--NFE correlation within each hardware group gives $\rho$ between 0.991 and
0.997 --- tighter than the pooled figure, so the confound had weakened the result rather than
manufactured it. The check is now part of the analysis script rather than a remark.

\subsubsection{Claim 7: NFE growth during training}

Measured on the convolutional ODE-Net at a tolerance that passes the reconstruction check on every
seed and epoch ($10^{-7}$), the forward NFE grows from \textbf{384 to 738} over six epochs, in
every one of five seeds. The claim reproduces.

The more interesting quantity is not the NFE but the tolerance. Over the same six epochs the
fraction of seeds passing the check at $10^{-5}$ falls from 1.0 to 0.4, while $10^{-6}$ and
$10^{-7}$ continue to hold: the field does not merely become more expensive to integrate, it
becomes harder to integrate \emph{at all} at a fixed tolerance. This is the mechanism behind
Section~\ref{sec:recon}, measured directly, and it is why a tolerance chosen at the start of
training cannot be assumed to remain adequate at the end. A companion ten-epoch run at a single
tolerance of $10^{-5}$ illustrates the trap: its NFE curve rises smoothly and looks entirely
reasonable, but by the final epoch none of its five seeds passes the check, so the right-hand half
of that curve does not measure an integrated flow.

\subsubsection{Claim 8: constant-memory adjoint}

The original's memory claim concerns the ODE-Net's own effective depth, but the natural-looking
experiment --- varying the depth of a discrete baseline and reporting the ODE-Net as a single point
--- tests the wrong axis, which is what an earlier version of this project did. The claim requires
holding the model fixed and driving \emph{its} number of function evaluations up.

Doing that by tightening the solver tolerance, peak memory under the adjoint is flat at
\textbf{$+0.0007$ MB per function evaluation} (442 MB across a fivefold increase in NFE), while
direct backpropagation through the solver rises at \textbf{$+31.8$ MB per function evaluation}
(689 MB to 3185 MB over the same range). The claim reproduces, and the two methods' gradients
agree throughout to within half a percent, so the constant memory is not bought with a worse
gradient. This experiment used three seeds rather than the five we use elsewhere; the effect is
four orders of magnitude and unambiguous in every seed, but we note the smaller sample.
