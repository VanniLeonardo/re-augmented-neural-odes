\section{Scope of reproducibility}
\label{sec:scope}

Dupont et al.\supercite{dupont2019augmented} argue that a Neural ODE is limited in a way that is
structural rather than statistical. The flow of an ODE is a homeomorphism of the state space, so
the map it can represent cannot alter the topology of the input: trajectories cannot cross, and
features that are nested or interleaved in the input cannot be pulled apart. Their remedy,
\emph{augmentation}, appends extra zero-valued dimensions to the state, giving the flow room to
separate the data in a higher-dimensional space. The paper supports this with a topological
argument, a cost signature (the number of function evaluations, NFE, that the adaptive solver
needs), and image benchmarks at matched parameter counts.

We test the following claims. Claims 1--4 are from Dupont et al.; claims 5--8 are the
solver and memory claims of Chen et al.\supercite{chen2018neural} on which the first paper builds.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A one-dimensional Neural ODE cannot represent the crossing map.} The flow of a
        scalar ODE is order-preserving, so no Neural ODE can approximate
        $g(x)=+1$ for $x<0$ and $g(x)=-1$ for $x>0$; a single augmented dimension suffices.
        (Proposition 1 and Figure 3.)
  \item \textbf{The cost of a Neural ODE grows as it trains, while an augmented one stays flat.}
        On a task requiring the flow to separate nested classes, the NFE required by the
        Neural ODE increases with the training budget, and the augmented model is both cheaper
        and flatter. (Section 4.2 and Figure 6.)
  \item \textbf{Neural ODEs generalise poorly across a region absent from training.} Removing an
        angular wedge from the training set leaves a large generalisation gap on that wedge for
        the Neural ODE, while the augmented model generalises across it.
        (Section 5.1 and Figure 9.)
  \item \textbf{At matched parameter counts, augmentation improves image accuracy and reduces
        cost.} On MNIST and CIFAR-10, the augmented model reaches higher test accuracy than a
        parameter-matched Neural ODE, at lower NFE. (Table 1.)
  \item \textbf{Solver error falls and cost rises as the tolerance tightens}, with forward
        computation time approximately proportional to NFE. (Chen et al., Figure 3a--b.)
  \item \textbf{The backward pass costs about half the forward pass in function evaluations.}
        (Chen et al., Figure 3c.)
  \item \textbf{The number of function evaluations increases over the course of training.}
        (Chen et al., Figure 3d.)
  \item \textbf{The adjoint method's memory cost is constant in the effective depth of the
        model.} (Chen et al., Table 1, memory column.)
\end{enumerate}

Claims 1--3 are qualitative and mechanistic; claims 4--8 are quantitative, and for those the
tolerance at which the ODE is solved determines whether the measurement means anything at all.
Section~\ref{sec:recon} makes that precise, because it governs every number we report.

We do not attempt Chen et al.'s Table 1 benchmark results: our MNIST models are our own seeded
baselines and are not comparable to that table's architecture, budget or solver. We also exclude
the Latent ODE and ODE-RNN models of Rubanova et al.\supercite{rubanova2019latent}, and the SVHN
and ImageNet experiments of the original paper, none of which are needed to test the claims
above and all of which exceeded the compute available to us.

\subsection{Solver tolerance and the reconstruction check}
\label{sec:recon}

An adaptive ODE solver takes a tolerance and returns a trajectory together with the number of
function evaluations it used. Neither output announces failure. If the tolerance is too loose for
the field being integrated, the solver still returns a plausible-looking state and a plausible
NFE, and every quantity derived from them --- accuracy, cost, the comparison between two models
--- describes the solver's shortcut rather than the model. This matters especially here, because
the vector field of a Neural ODE \emph{stiffens as it trains}: the tolerance that integrated the
field at initialisation need not integrate it later, so a fixed tolerance silently changes meaning
over the course of a run.

We therefore treat the solver tolerance as an experimental axis rather than a default, and attach
a check to every measurement. Because the flow of an ODE is invertible, integrating forward from
$0$ to $T$ and then back from $T$ to $0$ must return the input. We integrate in both directions
with the same solver and tolerance and record the relative reconstruction error
\[
  \varepsilon_{\text{recon}}
  \;=\;
  \frac{\lVert \phi_{T\rightarrow 0}(\phi_{0\rightarrow T}(x)) - x \rVert}{\lVert x \rVert},
\]
flagging a measurement as faithful (\texttt{recon\_ok}) when $\varepsilon_{\text{recon}} < 10^{-2}$.
This is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one: it detects a solver that is not tracking the
flow, and it is cheap enough to run on every row.

Two rules follow, and we apply them throughout. First, a quantity compared between two models is
reported at the \emph{loosest tolerance at which both models pass the check on every seed}, so
that neither is flattered by a tolerance that suits only it. Second, rows that fail the check are
kept and reported rather than discarded: they are evidence about the tolerance axis, and several
of our findings are visible only in them.

The consequences are not hypothetical. Measured at the solver library's default tolerance of
$10^{-3}$, our one-dimensional crossing-flow experiment fails the check for all ten trained
models, the worst reconstructing its input with a relative error above $10$; the conclusion
happens to survive re-measurement, but at that tolerance it had not been established. In the
image experiments the check fails at the tolerance used for \emph{training} on every model and
seed, which we return to in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Several conclusions reached earlier in
this project were retracted once the check was introduced.
